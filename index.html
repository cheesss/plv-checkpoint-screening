Abstract

What does each offline metric actually tell us?

Robot imitation learning commonly selects checkpoints with objective validation loss or action error because simulator and real-robot rollouts are expensive. These open-loop scores evaluate policies on expert states, but they do not represent policy-induced feedback or the moment an action-chunking policy replaces a committed plan.

We study a hierarchy of checkpoint evidence: objective loss, Action L1, dynamics-free temporal regularity, teacher-forced replanning, learned-model feedback, and simulator feedback. The primary cohort contains 1,365 low-dimensional robomimic checkpoints from 45 independent runs across five manipulation tasks, each evaluated with 50 simulator rollouts.

The result is structured heterogeneity rather than a universal winner. PLV-1 smoothness and Action L1 have similar task-balanced rank correlation, while cheap metrics retain lower selection regret. PLV-1 is stronger on Diffusion Policy but weaker on BC-RNN. Execution-matched replanning reveals a hard-task signal that pointwise action error misses, yet matched controls show that learned dynamics does not justify automatic reranking. PLV is therefore best understood as a temporal diagnostic for action-chunking regimes, not a universal replacement for Action L1.

Method

An evidence hierarchy, not another universal score

Every branch starts from the same policy checkpoint. What changes is the source of the next observation and, therefore, the question the score is allowed to answer.

01

Action error

Does one predicted action match the demonstration?

02

Teacher replan

Is the next plan consistent on recorded expert states?

03

Model feedback

Does predicted feedback cause the policy to revise its intent?

04

Simulator check

Does executed feedback produce the same disagreement?

Matched control

Accurate transition prediction is not enough

Demonstration prediction is accurate at the current model budget, but policy-induced multi-step drift grows sharply. On the same retained checkpoints, simulator-induced replanning has an adverse oriented correlation of −0.212.

The control separates three requirements that are often conflated: transition fidelity, model–simulator agreement, and preservation of checkpoint success order.

Disagreement requests a rollout. It does not automatically overturn the cheap ranking.

Takeaway

Use cheap metrics to screen competence. Use replanning diagnostics to expose disagreement. Request rollout when the evidence conflicts.

The supported contribution is a map of what each offline metric can decide, and where it should abstain.
